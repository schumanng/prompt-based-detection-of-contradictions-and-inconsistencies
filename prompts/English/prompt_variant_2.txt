From now on, please act as a classifier who has to decide for each pair of given sentences (hereinafter referred to as 'sentence pair') whether they contradict each other, are neutral to each other, are inconsistent, or have different contexts (so you have exactly four possible answers: 'Contradiction', 'Neutral', 'Inconsistent', and 'Different Context').
The following describes the conditions under which each of the four target classes applies to a given sentence pair.

target class: 'Contradiction':
In principle, a sentence pair is considered a 'contradiction' if the normative statements described in the two sentences cannot be true at the same time or cannot be followed at the same time, i.e., only either 'sentence 1' or 'sentence 2' is possible. The following rules are intended to assist in the classification of the pairs of sentences submitted:
   - Antonyms, negations, and mitigations: A contradiction exists when one of the two sentences reverses or mitigates the normative statement of the other sentence through antonyms, negations, or other terms, such that both statements cannot be followed simultaneously. In addition to verbs (often modal verbs) or adjectives, adverbs and nouns can also lead to a contradiction between two normative statements. Important: Qualifiers can also be indicated by terms such as 'if necessary,' 'where applicable,' 'where possible,' 'as far as possible,' 'ideally,' 'if possible,' etc., provided they appear in only one of the two sentences and refer to the relevant normative statement in that context.
   - Specifications without alternatives: A contradiction exists when one of the sentences presents a normative statement as the only possible option for a specific situation, condition, or group of addressees, while the other sentence provides for multiple alternatives. In the case of permissions, such contradictions are typically marked by strict adjectives and adverbs such as 'only,' 'exclusively,' etc., and apply even if only one of the two sentences contains them. In the case of obligations, however, they apply even without strict adjectives or when it is merely implied that there are alternatives to the prescribed instruction.
   - Same specification, more extensive conditions: A contradiction exists when both sentences convey a semantically identical normative statement, but one sentence attaches more extensive (pre)conditions to it than the other.
   - Contradictory numerical object characterization: A contradiction exists when both sentences have the same subject or condition, but the numerical values characterizing the (also identical) objects in question differ in the two sentences.
   - Contradictory numerical subject characterization: A contradiction exists when both sentences have a semantically identical normative statement, but the numerical values characterizing the (equally identical) subjects of the sentences differ. Such statements cannot coexist, since they do not refer to different variants of the same subject, but to exactly the same subjects, which are conditioned differently by the numerical values.
   - Conflicting (individual) objects: A conflict exists when different provisions are referenced or apply to exactly the same situation or condition, such that the respective requirements cannot be complied with simultaneously.

target class: 'Inconsistent':
Generally, a sentence pair is considered 'Inconsistent' if a comparison of the two statements makes sense because they address the same topic (the same context), but their essential normative statements are inconsistent without directly contradicting each other. This means that both statements/regulations could be followed simultaneously without one making the other impossible. Nevertheless, a discrepancy remains that is relevant from a regulatory perspective. The target class 'Inconsistent' thus represents a precursor to contradiction and can be interpreted and classified based on the following four distinguishing features:
   - Additional situations or addressees: An inconsistency exists when one of the two sentences specifies more situations or addressees for the same action than the other sentence does. If the sentence does not contain strict terms that lead to a restriction (e.g., 'the action applies only in situation XY' or only to addressee group Z), then these statements do not contradict each other, but differ in the scope of their application.
   - Same situation, more detailed information: An inconsistency exists when one of the two sentences contains a more specific instruction for the same situation or condition, thereby leaving the person concerned less leeway in complying with the respective instruction. Important: An inconsistency also exists if relevant additional information is provided in parentheses (however, it is important that it is not preceded by “e.g.” or “for example” and that only one of the two sentences includes this relevant additional information).
   - Deviating partial information: If both statements do indeed overlap in terms of identical information/requirements, but a discrepancy remains that may affect compliance with the respective requirement (i.e., the information is not more precise—as in the condition “same facts, more detailed information”—but deviates in certain aspects). In many cases, a discrepancy in partial information (and thus an inconsistency) exists, particularly when certain terms differ in a list within the two sentences or when one of the two sentences lists more terms than the other.
   - Part-whole relationship through generic terms: An inconsistency exists when both sentences are directed at a specific addressee or situation (subject), but one of the sentences uses a generic term for the other subject or encompasses all subtypes of the other subject.

target class: 'Neutral':
Basically, a sentence pair is considered 'neutral' if a comparison of the two sentences makes sense because they deal with the same topic (the same context) and are neither contradictory nor inconsistent. The target class 'neutral' can be interpreted and classified using the following distinguishing features (important: there are a few exceptions in this category, which are also described below):
   - Neutral words or phrases: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if one of the two sentences contains additional neutral words or phrases that do not appear in the other sentence. However, it is crucial that the words or phrases do not have a restrictive influence on the interpretation or compliance with the respective normative statements, nor do they expand the statements or requirements in such a way that this would result in an inconsistency with the other sentence. In other words, it is possible that even a single differing word can create an inconsistency if it does not appear in the other sentence and thereby actually noticeably alters or expands the original statement (e.g., if the word “in writing” appears in only one of the two sentences!). So, the important thing to remember is: When in doubt (i.e., when the implications of the supposedly neutral word or phrase are difficult to assess), you should opt for the 'Inconsistent' target class!
   - Independent normative subordinate clauses: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if one of the two sentences contains a subordinate clause that makes an additional normative statement, but one that does not influence, further specify, or concretize the main statement (which the other sentence also addresses), and can instead be considered separately from it. This is typically indicated by the presence of an additional situation or subject in the sentence (often in combination with a different verb). However, the following applies here: If the supposed independence of the subordinate clause—and thus its impact on the main statement—is difficult to assess, you should opt for the 'Inconsistent' target class!
   - Use of (additional) examples: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if one of the two sentences supplements a normative statement with additional examples, while the other sentence contains no examples, fewer examples, or different examples. Such cases must be classified as 'neutral' because the normative statement itself is not altered, but merely explained in more detail through examples of the respective entities/objects.
   - Mentioning an option as an example: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if one of the two sentences lists several possibilities/options for handling a situation and the other sentence uses one of these options as an example. However, if one of the options is mentioned not as an example but as a specific instruction, the sentence pairs must be classified as a 'contradiction.'
   - Synonyms and Abbreviations: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if one of the sentences uses clear synonyms or abbreviations for terms found in the other sentence.
   - Justifications vs. No Justifications: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if one of the two sentences provides additional justification or rationale for a normative statement, while the other does not. Such cases can be interpreted as 'neutral' because this additional information is not essential for compliance with the relevant regulation.
   - Different justifications: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if both sentences provide a justification, but the justifications differ. The assumption: As long as the actual normative statement is identical, the justification should be of secondary importance.
   - Potential and actual conditions: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if one of the two sentences attaches a (pre)condition to a specific normative statement that is already considered fulfilled in the other sentence.
   - General statements: A sentence pairs is considered “neutral” if one of the two sentences makes a normative statement directed at a specific addressee (subject) or a specific situation, while the other sentence expresses the same statement in a completely general way, so that it applies to all addressees or situations. In any case, however, it is crucial that the sentence specifically naming the addressees/situations does not do so in a strict/restrictive manner (e.g., 'Only employees must...'), as this would otherwise contradict the universal statement of the other sentence. Therefore, the same rule applies here: If the scope of the supposed general validity is difficult to assess, you should opt for the 'Inconsistent' target class!
   - Support for locating objects and entities: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if only one of the two sentences specifies where the referenced subjects, objects, or other entities are located in the real world. However, such cases should only be marked as neutral if this information does not alter the normative statement in question, but merely provides the reader with additional context.
   - Divergent cross-references: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if both sentences refer to different locations where the (identical) normative statements appear in the respective regulatory document or where further information can be found. This rule is based on our basic assumption that the two sentences provided originate from different regulatory documents. This inevitably leads to cases where the sentences refer to different locations within their respective documents, simply because the documents may be structured differently.
   - Contrasting subject and contrasting object: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if the two sentences have contrasting subject phrases, but the respective object phrases are also contrasting and are therefore 'neutral' in relation to each other.

target class: 'Different Context':
A sentence pair must be classified as 'Different Context' if a comparison of the two sentences does not make sense because they do not address the same topic. This does not mean that the two sentences are unrelated, but rather that the inherent essential normative statement within the overarching topic differs between the two sentences, and they are therefore not related to one another. The target class 'Different Context' can be interpreted and classified based on the following distinguishing features:
   - Divergent subject characteristics: A sentence pairs is considered a “different context” if the essential normative statement (e.g., a requirement, measure, obligation, etc.) is identical in both sentences, but the specific subject (situation, entity, addressee, condition, etc.) to which the normative statement refers differs in certain respects. These cases frequently occur in a regulatory context and particularly when comparing different documents, but they must not automatically lead to conflict or inconsistency reports, since both normative statements may refer to multiple subjects or situations simultaneously; that is, they may apply at the same time as long as they do not contradict each other.
   - (Single) differing verb and no object: A sentence pairs is considered a 'Different Context' if there is no object in the sentence and the subject or condition is the same in both sentences, but the (only verb present) that acts upon it is different. Since the object is missing, the verb stands as the 'sole action' for the respective context in which the sentence is to be interpreted. If these verbs differ, the two sentences are no longer in the same context and can therefore apply simultaneously, i.e., they can be true at the same time.
   - Different contextual nuances: A sentence pairs is considered a 'different context' if the subject (or situation) and an action relating to the subject are identical, but the context linking them is different. This would be the case, for example, if two sentences make normative statements about the collection and processing of personal data, but one sentence refers to the 'how' and the other to the 'what for'.
   - Uncertainty regarding coreference: When the subject of one sentence is most likely a specific instance of the subject in the other sentence, but this cannot be stated with 100% certainty (at least not without knowing the surrounding context).

Important general information: When making your classification, please take all of the rules described into account at the same time, i.e., always ensure that no other rules or conditions are violated. Feel free to take some time to think about your decision.
Please only give the name of the respective target class as your answer (i.e., either 'Contradiction,' 'Inconsistent,' 'Neutral,' or 'Different Context')!
