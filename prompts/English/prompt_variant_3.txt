From now on, please act as a classifier who has to decide for each pair of given sentences (hereinafter referred to as 'sentence pair') whether they contradict each other, are neutral to each other, are inconsistent, or have different contexts (so you have exactly four possible answers: 'Contradiction', 'Neutral', 'Inconsistent', and 'Different Context').
The following describes the conditions under which each of the four target classes applies to a given sentence pair.

target class: 'Contradiction':
In principle, a sentence pair is considered a 'contradiction' if the normative statements described in the two sentences cannot be true at the same time or cannot be followed at the same time, i.e., only either 'sentence 1' or 'sentence 2' is possible. The following rules are intended to assist in the classification of the pairs of sentences submitted:
   - Antonyms, negations, and mitigations: A contradiction exists when one of the two sentences reverses or mitigates the normative statement of the other sentence through antonyms, negations, or other terms, such that both statements cannot be followed simultaneously (e.g., Sentence 1: 'If necessary, the supplier may issue an order confirmation,' Sentence 2: 'The supplier must issue an order confirmation in all cases.'). In addition to verbs (often modal verbs) or adjectives, adverbs and nouns can also lead to a contradiction between two normative statements. An example of a qualification, on the other hand, would be: Sentence 1: 'All welding and cutting work must be separated from other activities in terms of space and time,' Sentence 2: 'Welding and cutting work should be separated from other activities in terms of space and time whenever possible.' (Important: Qualifiers can also be indicated by terms such as 'if necessary,' 'where applicable,' 'where possible,' 'as far as possible,' 'ideally,' 'if possible,' etc., provided they appear in only one of the two sentences and refer to the relevant normative statement in that context. Example 1: Sentence 1: 'The result of the audit is communicated to the supplier, if applicable, in the form of a written audit report.' Sentence 2: 'The result of the audit is communicated to the supplier in the form of a written audit report.' Example 2: Sentence 1: 'Certification requirements: The supplier undertakes to implement environmental management systems at its facilities that meet the requirements of ISO 14001.' Sentence 2: 'The supplier must establish and maintain an environmental management system that ideally complies with ISO 14001 or EMAS requirements and can be certified if necessary. '). A softening of the requirement may also be provided, for example, by an exception clause mentioned in only one of the two sentences (e.g., Sentence 1: 'Employees must maintain confidentiality regarding all trade and business secrets both during the term of the employment relationship and after its termination, unless expressly permitted in advance by law or by the company.' , Sentence 2: 'All trade and business secrets must be kept confidential both during the term of employment and thereafter.'). Also important: the verbs 'should' / 'ought to' and 'must' are NOT antonyms (and therefore do not automatically lead to a contradiction). Similarly, the word combinations 'shall not' and 'must not' are NOT antonyms (the following sentence pairs would therefore NOT be a contradiction: Example: Sentence 1: 'Personal items not required for work must not be brought into the workplace.'; Sentence 2: 'Personal items not required for work should not be brought into the workplace.').
   - Specifications without alternatives: A contradiction exists when one of the sentences presents a normative statement as the only possible option for a specific situation, condition, or group of addressees, while the other sentence provides for multiple alternatives. In the case of permissions, such contradictions are typically marked by strict adjectives and adverbs such as 'only,' 'exclusively,' etc., and apply even if only one of the two sentences contains them (e.g., Sentence 1: 'Employees of partner companies may park their cars exclusively in the parking garage to the left of the main gate,' Sentence 2: 'Employees of partner companies may park their cars in the parking garage to the left of the main gate or in the outdoor parking lot to the right of the driveway.'). In the case of obligations, however, they apply even without strict adjectives (e.g., Sentence 1: 'All rules must be included in the facility logbook,' Sentence 2: 'All rules must be included in the site logbook or in the Betra.') or when it is merely implied that there are alternatives to the prescribed instruction (e.g., Sentence 1: 'The on-site technical briefing must be documented in the written work permit,' Sentence 2: 'The on-site technical briefing must be documented (e.g., in the written work permit).').
   - Same specification, more extensive conditions: A contradiction exists when both sentences convey a semantically identical normative statement, but one sentence attaches more extensive (pre)conditions to it than the other (e.g., Sentence 1: 'The components are approved for installation provided that no defects are found during the identification inspection,' Sentence 2: 'If no defects are found during the identification check and the control checks, the components are approved for installation.').
   - Contradictory numerical object characterization: A contradiction exists when both sentences have the same subject or condition, but the numerical values characterizing the (also identical) objects in question differ in the two sentences (e.g., Sentence 1: 'Invalid specification documents are subject to a retention period of at least 2 years,' Sentence 2: 'Invalid specification documents must be retained for at least 1.5 years.').
   - Contradictory numerical subject characterization: A contradiction exists when both sentences have a semantically identical normative statement, but the numerical values characterizing the (equally identical) subjects of the sentences differ (e.g., Sentence 1: 'If the defect rate is less than 3%, consultation with the supplier takes place to determine whether this damage should also be treated as a serial defect.'; Sentence 2: 'If the defect rate is < 2.5%, consultation with the supplier takes place to clarify whether this damage should also be considered a serial defect.'). Such statements cannot coexist, since they do not refer to different variants of the same subject, but to exactly the same subjects, which are conditioned differently by the numerical values.
   - Conflicting (individual) objects: A conflict exists when different provisions are referenced or apply to exactly the same situation or condition, such that the respective requirements cannot be complied with simultaneously (e.g., Sentence 1: 'All cases of violations of the regulations on the protection of personal data must be reported to the data protection coordinator without delay,' Sentence 2: 'Should violations of the regulations on the protection of personal data come to light, these must be reported immediately to management.').

target class: 'Inconsistent':
Generally, a sentence pair is considered 'Inconsistent' if a comparison of the two statements makes sense because they address the same topic (the same context), but their essential normative statements are inconsistent without directly contradicting each other. This means that both statements/regulations could be followed simultaneously without one making the other impossible. Nevertheless, a discrepancy remains that is relevant from a regulatory perspective. The target class 'Inconsistent' thus represents a precursor to contradiction and can be interpreted and classified based on the following four distinguishing features:
   - Additional situations or addressees: An inconsistency exists when one of the two sentences specifies more situations or addressees for the same action than the other sentence does. If the sentence does not contain strict terms that lead to a restriction (e.g., 'the action applies only in situation XY' or only to addressee group Z), then these statements do not contradict each other, but differ in the scope of their application. An example of an inconsistent sentence pairs due to 'additional situations or addressees' is: Sentence 1: 'When handling flammable liquids, all ignition sources must be avoided.' Sentence 2: 'When handling flammable liquids and in potentially explosive areas, ignition sources must be avoided.' Another example would be: Sentence 1: 'In the event of a noticeable odor, dust, feeling unwell, an alarm, or special difficulties, work must be stopped immediately.' Sentence 2: 'Work must be stopped immediately if there is a noticeable odor, if you feel unwell, in the event of an alarm, or if there are special difficulties.' Third example: Sentence 1: 'Hearing protection must be worn in designated noise areas.' Sentence 2: 'Hearing protection must be worn in designated noise areas or during noisy activities.' Quarter example: Sentence 1: 'Upon completion of the construction and installation work or upon expiration of the contract term, all equipment must be dismantled and removed from the plant.' Sentence 2: 'Upon completion of the construction and installation work, the contractor must dismantle all equipment and remove it from the plant.'
   - Same situation, more detailed information: An inconsistency exists when one of the two sentences contains a more specific instruction for the same situation or condition, thereby leaving the person concerned less leeway in complying with the respective instruction. This can result from even minor but (regulatorily) relevant differences. Important: An inconsistency also exists if relevant additional information is provided in parentheses (however, it is important that it is not preceded by “e.g.” or “for example” and that only one of the two sentences includes this relevant additional information): Example 26: Sentence 1: “The user must check the safe operating condition of the work equipment used every working day.”, Sentence 2: 'The user must check the safe operating condition of the work equipment used every working day (by visual inspection before starting work).', Example 27: Sentence 1: 'The storage and use of hazardous substances must be reported to the coordinator in advance.', Sentence 2: 'The storage and use of hazardous substances must be reported to the coordinator in advance (via a safety data sheet).'
   - Deviating partial information: If both statements do indeed overlap in terms of identical information/requirements, but a discrepancy remains that may affect compliance with the respective requirement (i.e., the information is not more precise—as in the condition “same facts, more detailed information”—but deviates in certain aspects). Example: Sentence 1: 'Every employee shall immediately report any violations of this privacy policy or other regulations regarding the protection of personal data to their respective supervisor, data protection coordinator, or data protection officer.' In many cases, a discrepancy in partial information (and thus an inconsistency) exists, particularly when certain terms differ in a list within the two sentences or when one of the two sentences lists more terms than the other (Example 1: Sentence 1: 'Discrimination is prohibited, in particular on the basis of a person's ethnic origin, religion, or beliefs, disability, age, sexual identity, or gender.' Sentence 2: 'No discrimination on the basis of age, gender, religion, national or ethnic origin, marital status, disability, culture, political opinion, sexual orientation, or social affiliation will be tolerated.' Example 2: Sentence 1: 'Personal data may only be collected, processed, and used for predetermined, lawful purposes, and only to the extent necessary to achieve the respective purpose.' Sentence 2: 'Personal data may only be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and may not be further processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes.').
   - Part-whole relationship through generic terms: An inconsistency exists when both sentences are directed at a specific addressee or situation (subject), but one of the sentences uses a generic term for the other subject or encompasses all subtypes of the other subject (e.g., Sentence 1: 'The processing of personal data is also permissible if state laws require, presuppose, or permit data processing.'; Sentence 2: 'The processing of employees' personal data is also permissible if state laws require, presuppose, or permit data processing.').

target class: 'Neutral':
Basically, a sentence pair is considered 'neutral' if a comparison of the two sentences makes sense because they deal with the same topic (the same context) and are neither contradictory nor inconsistent. The target class 'neutral' can be interpreted and classified using the following distinguishing features (important: there are a few exceptions in this category, which are also described below):
   - Neutral words or phrases: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if one of the two sentences contains additional neutral words or phrases that do not appear in the other sentence (Example 1: Sentence 1: 'The traffic regulations apply throughout the entire premises.'; Sentence 2: 'The traffic regulations apply on the premises.'; Example 2: Sentence 1: 'The applicable value according to Module 408.1101 must be used as the reference slope.', Sentence 2: 'In Section 21, Paragraph 1, it was added that the applicable value of the reference slope according to Module 408.1101 must be used.', Example 3: Sentence 1: 'The supplier agrees to enter into a quality assurance agreement with Mustermann AG.', Sentence 2: 'The supplier agrees in principle to conclude a quality assurance agreement with Mustermann AG.'; Example 4: Sentence 1: 'No employee shall suffer any disadvantages as a result of disclosing information.'; Sentence 2: 'No employee shall suffer any disadvantages as a result of disclosing information in this respect.'). However, it is crucial that the words or phrases do not have a restrictive influence on the interpretation or compliance with the respective normative statements, nor do they expand the statements or requirements in such a way that this would result in an inconsistency with the other sentence. In other words, it is possible that even a single differing word can create an inconsistency if it does not appear in the other sentence and thereby actually noticeably alters or expands the original statement (e.g., if the word “in writing” appears in only one of the two sentences!). So, the important thing to remember is: When in doubt (i.e., when the implications of the supposedly neutral word or phrase are difficult to assess), you should opt for the 'Inconsistent' target class!
   - Independent normative subordinate clauses: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if one of the two sentences contains a subordinate clause that makes an additional normative statement, but one that does not influence, further specify, or concretize the main statement (which the other sentence also addresses), and can instead be considered separately from it. This is typically indicated by the presence of an additional situation or subject in the sentence (often in combination with a different verb). (Example 1: Sentence 1: 'The employee ID card must not be given to a third party.'; Sentence 2: 'The employee ID card must not be given to a third party and must be returned upon leaving the company or the organization.'; Example 2: Sentence 1: 'The reduction will be taken into account in the next budget allocation.'; Sentence 2: 'The reduction will be taken into account in the next budget allocation, and the amount of the reduction will be determined based on various factors (severity, extent, frequency, etc.).'). However, the following applies here: If the supposed independence of the subordinate clause—and thus its impact on the main statement—is difficult to assess, you should opt for the 'Inconsistent' target class!
   - Use of (additional) examples: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if one of the two sentences supplements a normative statement with additional examples, while the other sentence contains no examples, fewer examples, or different examples. Such cases must be classified as 'neutral' because the normative statement itself is not altered, but merely explained in more detail through examples of the respective entities/objects (Example 1: Sentence 1: 'Work at elevated locations must be secured by protective measures, such as parapets or railings.' Sentence 2: 'Workplaces at heights must be secured by protective measures, such as parapets, safety nets, or railings.', Example 2: Sentence 1: 'Legitimate interests are generally based on legal grounds (e.g., the assertion, exercise, or defense of legal claims) or economic grounds (e.g., the valuation of companies).', Sentence 2: 'Legitimate interests are generally based on legal grounds (e.g., the assertion, exercise, or defense of legal claims) or economic grounds (e.g., employee evaluation).', Example 3: Sentence 1: 'Mustermann AG Standard 9666 can be referenced in its entirety by specifying the standard number “Mustermann AG Standard 9666 (all parts)” without naming a specific part, or a specific part can be referenced individually, e.g., as “Mustermann AG Standard 9666 - 4”.', Sentence 2: 'The Mustermann AG Standard 9666 can be referenced in its entirety by specifying the standard number “Mustermann AG Standard 9666 (all parts)” without naming a specific section, or a specific section can be referenced individually, e.g., as “Mustermann AG Standard 9666 - 6”.' Example 4: Sentence 1: 'The special characteristics must be identified in all relevant product and process documents, such as drawings, FMEA, risk analyses, work plans, test plans, and production control plans.' Sentence 2: 'The special features must be identified in all relevant product and process documents, such as drawings, test plans, FMEA, and production control plans.' Example 5: Sentence 1: 'The supplier must ensure, through preventive maintenance, that the tools, machines, and equipment are functional and ready for use at all times.' Sentence 2: 'Through preventive maintenance (e.g., in accordance with VDI 2890), the supplier ensures that the tools, machines, and equipment are functional and ready for use at all times.' In the second example (in Sentence 2), an example was added in parentheses showing how preventive maintenance could be carried out, namely 'in accordance with VDI 2890'. It is important that this parenthetical content is actually mentioned as an example; that is, if the addition 'e.g.' or 'for example' were missing, the target class 'Inconsistent' would apply instead. The same rule (i.e., that you generally need to check whether “e.g.” or “for example” precedes the additional information) naturally also applies to cases where the additional information is not provided in parentheses.
   - Mentioning an option as an example: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if one of the two sentences lists several possibilities/options for handling a situation and the other sentence uses one of these options as an example (e.g., Sentence 1: 'If the order to the supplier includes development tasks, the requirements shall be specified in writing by the contracting parties, in the form of a specification sheet or technical data sheet (or similar).' , Sentence 2: 'Should the order to the supplier include development tasks, the requirements shall be specified in writing by the contracting parties, e.g., in the form of a specification sheet.'). However, if one of the options is mentioned not as an example but as a specific instruction, the sentence pairs must be classified as a 'contradiction.'
   - Synonyms and Abbreviations: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if one of the sentences uses clear synonyms or abbreviations for terms found in the other sentence (Example 1: Sentence 1: 'The FMEA must be continuously updated to reflect any errors that have occurred, newly identified risks, defect reports, process changes, etc.' Sentence 2: 'The FMEA must be continuously updated with regard to errors that have occurred, newly identified risks, complaints, process changes, etc.,' Example 2: Sentence 1: 'For standardized surveys, the survey form must be submitted to the DPO for approval,' Sentence 2: 'For standardized surveys, the survey form must be submitted to the Data Protection Officer for approval.' Example 3: Sentence 1: 'The initial samples must be manufactured under production conditions and tested for all quality characteristics required in the technical specifications,' Sentence 2: 'The initial samples must be manufactured under production conditions and tested for all quality characteristics required in the technical documentation.'). IMPORTANT: This rule does not apply to generic terms! Also important: If you are unsure about a potential synonym or abbreviation, it is better to choose the target class 'Inconsistent'.
   - Justifications vs. No Justifications: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if one of the two sentences provides additional justification or rationale for a normative statement, while the other does not. Such cases can be interpreted as 'neutral' because this additional information is not essential for compliance with the relevant regulation (Example 1: Sentence 1: 'Prototypes must be clearly marked with tags and stickers.' , Sentence 2: 'Prototypes must be clearly marked with tags and stickers so that they can be distinguished from production parts.' Example 2: Sentence 1: 'The information security officer must be involved in all projects at an early stage so that security-related aspects can be taken into account as early as the planning phase.'; Sentence 2: 'The information security officer must be involved in all projects at an early stage.').
   - Different justifications: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if both sentences provide a justification, but the justifications differ. The assumption: As long as the actual normative statement is identical, the justification should be of secondary importance (Example 1: Sentence 1: 'If possible, outer packaging/disposable packaging (e.g., films, bags, nonwovens, etc.) should be avoided in packaging planning to prevent inefficient handling steps.' Sentence 2: 'To avoid inefficient handling steps and waste of resources, outer packaging/single-use packaging should be avoided during packaging planning (if possible).', Example 2: Sentence 2: 'The supplier shall ensure that the goods are delivered in suitable transport containers to prevent damage and quality deterioration (e.g., contamination, chemical reactions).', Sentence 2: 'The supplier shall ensure that the goods are delivered in suitable transport containers to prevent damage and quality deterioration (e.g., contamination, chemical reactions, ESD …)'
   - Potential and actual conditions: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if one of the two sentences attaches a (pre)condition to a specific normative statement that is already considered fulfilled in the other sentence (e.g., Sentence 1: 'Based on the deadlines specified by Mustermann AG, the supplier must create a project-specific schedule and make it available to Mustermann AG.' , Sentence 2: 'Provided that Mustermann AG specifies deadlines for the supplier, the supplier shall draw up a project-related schedule and make it available to Mustermann AG.').
   - General statements: A sentence pairs is considered “neutral” if one of the two sentences makes a normative statement directed at a specific addressee (subject) or a specific situation, while the other sentence expresses the same statement in a completely general way, so that it applies to all addressees or situations (Example 1: Sentence 1: 'All trade and business secrets must be kept confidential both during the term of employment and after its termination.'; Sentence 2: 'Employees must keep all trade and business secrets confidential both during the term of employment and thereafter.'; Example 2: Sentence 1: 'Instruct your employees before they begin work for the first time.'; Sentence 2: 'Train the employees responsible for loading operations before they begin work for the first time.'). In any case, however, it is crucial that the sentence specifically naming the addressees/situations does not do so in a strict/restrictive manner (e.g., 'Only employees must...'), as this would otherwise contradict the universal statement of the other sentence. Therefore, the same rule applies here: If the scope of the supposed general validity is difficult to assess, you should opt for the 'Inconsistent' target class!
   - Support for locating objects and entities: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if only one of the two sentences specifies where the referenced subjects, objects, or other entities are located in the real world. However, such cases should only be marked as neutral if this information does not alter the normative statement in question, but merely provides the reader with additional context (e.g., Sentence 1: 'Change requests must be submitted by the supplier in writing using the Product-Change-Notification form.'; Sentence 2: 'The supplier must submit change requests in writing using the Product-Change-Notification form (see the Supplier Portal under the “Special Approvals” menu).')
   - Divergent cross-references: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if both sentences refer to different locations where the (identical) normative statements appear in the respective regulatory document or where further information can be found. This rule is based on our basic assumption that the two sentences provided originate from different regulatory documents. This inevitably leads to cases where the sentences refer to different locations within their respective documents, simply because the documents may be structured differently (Example 1: Sentence 1: 'No later than 10 business days before a certificate expires, the holder of that certificate must have made the successor certificate available (see Chapter 9).', Sentence 2: 'The holder of a certificate must have provided the successor certificate no later than 10 business days before it expires (see Chapter 7).', Example 2: Sentence 1: 'Opening the IT device and making changes to the hardware (e.g., installing or removing hard drives, memory modules) as well as manually changing security settings (e.g., browser settings) is permitted only by the authorized departments (see Appendix, B. 1.14).', Sentence 2: 'Opening the IT device and making changes to the hardware (e.g., installing or removing hard drives, memory modules) as well as manually changing security settings (e.g., browser settings) is permitted only by the authorized departments (see Appendix, A. 4.4).', Example 3: 'Information regarding shipping procedures can be found in the shipping instructions for parcel shipping in the Mustermann AG Supplier Portal (see Section 1.15) and must be strictly adhered to.' Sentence 2: 'Information regarding shipping procedures can be found in the shipping instructions for parcel shipping in the Mustermann AG Supplier Portal (see Section 1.16) and must be strictly adhered to.').
   - Contrasting subject and contrasting object: A sentence pairs is considered 'neutral' if the two sentences have contrasting subject phrases, but the respective object phrases are also contrasting and are therefore 'neutral' in relation to each other (e.g., Sentence 1: 'Mobile scaffolding may only be moved once no one is on it,' Sentence 2: 'Mobile scaffolding must not be moved if there are people on it.'). Keep in mind with this rule that such cases are most likely very rare.

target class: 'Different Context':
A sentence pair must be classified as 'Different Context' if a comparison of the two sentences does not make sense because they do not address the same topic. This does not mean that the two sentences are unrelated, but rather that the inherent essential normative statement within the overarching topic differs between the two sentences, and they are therefore not related to one another. The target class 'Different Context' can be interpreted and classified based on the following distinguishing features:
   - Divergent subject characteristics: A sentence pairs is considered a “different context” if the essential normative statement (e.g., a requirement, measure, obligation, etc.) is identical in both sentences, but the specific subject (situation, entity, addressee, condition, etc.) to which the normative statement refers differs in certain respects. These cases frequently occur in a regulatory context and particularly when comparing different documents, but they must not automatically lead to conflict or inconsistency reports, since both normative statements may refer to multiple subjects or situations simultaneously; that is, they may apply at the same time as long as they do not contradict each other. An example of a sentence pairs with differing subject characteristics is: Sentence 1: 'The use of heat exchangers made of aluminum materials is not permitted.'; Sentence 2: 'The use of heat exchangers made of plastic is not permitted.'
   - (Single) differing verb and no object: A sentence pairs is considered a 'Different Context' if there is no object in the sentence and the subject or condition is the same in both sentences, but the (only verb present) that acts upon it is different. Since the object is missing, the verb stands as the 'sole action' for the respective context in which the sentence is to be interpreted. If these verbs differ, the two sentences are no longer in the same context and can therefore apply simultaneously, i.e., they can be true at the same time (e.g., Sentence 1: 'Compliance with data protection regulations is monitored.'; Sentence 2: 'Compliance with data protection regulations is logged.').
   - Different contextual nuances: A sentence pairs is considered a 'different context' if the subject (or situation) and an action relating to the subject are identical, but the context linking them is different.
